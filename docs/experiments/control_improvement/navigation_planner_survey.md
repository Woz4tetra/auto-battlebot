# Navigation architecture for a 3 lb combat robot: planner and controller tradeoffs

Written 2026-09-20 from a two-pass deep-research run. The first pass fetched 27 sources and extracted 134 claims, then hit account rate limits during verification and never synthesized. This pass re-read every one of those sources, matched the orphaned claims back to them, added six angles the first pass never reached (cost-to-go and lattice planners, pursuit-evasion, published autonomous combat robots, small-budget optimizers on ARM, a moving hazard with a dropping track, learned policies), had a second agent check every claim against the fetched text, and ran a critic over the draft. Record: 56 sources, 256 usable claims, 0 refuted, 16 unverified and tagged. Four first-run claim groups lost their URLs and stay unverified ([G3], [G4], [G8], [G10]). The seed, evidence record and per-agent journal are in the session scratchpad, not in the repo.

Citations read `[S# conf]`: a claim in the verified record plus its confidence (high: primary source, confirmed or newly extracted; medium: corrected by the verifier, drawn from a verifier note, or a secondary source; low: unverified). `[Q]` is a number from the research question, `[R]` a repo document. `[G3]`, `[G4]`, `[G8]`, `[G10]` are first-run seed groups with lost URLs; everything from them is unverified. Numbers marked (derived) are arithmetic on a source's figures, not statements the source makes.

## 1. Recommendation

Build the new type as a two-rate plant-rollout planner: 50 to 150 structured candidate command sequences (two or three segments over 0.4 to 0.6 s) rolled through the fitted drivetrain from the delay-predicted state, scored against time-indexed hazard discs that grow with lookahead and against the opponent's predicted weapon sector, replanned at perception rate, and executed at 250 Hz by the existing inverse-plant tracker with `HazardAvoidance::limit_command` as the last layer. Three facts decide it. Sampling MPC on the Orin CPU is too slow at useful sample counts: Nav2's CPU MPPI takes 2.7 ms for 128 samples and 43.8 ms for 2048 over 100 steps on a Jetson Orin Nano [S2 high], and GPU MPPI on an Orin NX tops out at 66.7 Hz [S6 high] on a GPU this system does not have free. The RoboCup SSL champion replaced RRT with this kind of trajectory sampler; the 2019 version measured 0.3 ms average per iteration in the JVM [S20 high], and the 2024 champion version, which capped opponent lookahead at 0.5 s and switched to lazy acceptance, reports no timing [S14 high]. Its cost is linear in candidates times steps. The arena is 2.4 m with a handful of discs [Q], so no global layer earns its cost, and grid cost-to-go fields cannot carry a heading-dependent sector cost without an anisotropic solver whose compute grows with the anisotropy [S32 high]. Keep small-K SMPPI as the upgrade path on the same rollout and cost code [S4 high][S46 high]; do not start with nonlinear MPC, whose Orin NX evidence is box-constrained only [S47 medium], or a learned policy, since residual RL gave no real-world gain over its model-based base on a small car [S54 high].

One dependency shapes the rollout: the predictor and the plant adaptation need the EKF arm of the motion estimator. The shared config already selects it (`our_robot_mode = "EKF"` in `config/_common.toml`), although the plant fit has not yet passed the EKF acceptance criteria [R stage4_sim_report.md], and the Mr Stabs Mk2 profiles run `DeadReckoningMotionEstimator` because that robot has no jig fit [R plant_backed_control.md]. On a dead-reckoning arm Rank 1 degrades to open-loop feedforward with adaptation off, which is still enough to start the sweep.

## 3. Method families

### 3.1 Sampling MPC (MPPI and variants)

Compute. Nav2's CPU MPPI (differential drive, costmap cost, 100 steps) takes 2.7 ms at 128 samples, 5.1 at 256, 10.1 at 512 and 43.8 at 2048 on a Jetson Orin Nano, linear in samples [S2 high], about 200 ns per rollout step on the ARM cores (derived from [S2]). An Eigen plus OpenMP CPU MPPI on the same six A78 cores took 17.37 ms per step with K and N unreported [S3 high]. Nav2 sets model_dt to the control period, which at 4 ms shrinks a 56-step horizon to 0.22 s [S1 high]; decoupling planner rate from tick rate is the fix.

Moving obstacles and uncertainty. DC-MPPI samples 25 obstacle trajectories per obstacle once per step and adds a probability-weighted, time-indexed collision indicator, scaling O(P+N); collisions fell from 3 to 0 among 100 moving obstacles at 454 versus 283 ms per step in MATLAB [S5 high]. Ensemble variance in the running cost avoids CVaR's extra samples [S24 high]. Biased-MPPI gives one sample to each of J ancillary controllers and reached zero collisions at every K from 50 to 2000 where plain MPPI had 4 to 10 [S46 high]; this matters at small K because the weighted average of two good modes can land in the high-cost gap between them [S5 high]. More moving agents make the costmap noisier and trap the sampler in local minima, and sampling MPC has no stability guarantee [S45 high].

Heading-sector cost. Any per-step cost is admissible, so a cone test on the predicted opponent heading costs a few operations per step. No source demonstrates one.

Delay and lag. The rollout model can carry the delay buffer and asymmetric lags. F-MPPI ran a 50 Hz solve with locally linear feedback gains applied at 200 Hz, matched plain MPPI at 100 Hz on disturbance rejection and produced smoother commands [S6 high]; plain MPPI on the same Orin NX GPU maxed at 66.7 Hz [S6 high]. In simulation with a randomized unknown system delay t_d, meta-initialized model adaptation still cut lateral error from 1.12 m to 0.22 m [S24 high], so an unmodeled delay is survivable when the learned model absorbs it.

Online adaptation. MPPI absorbs model error: on an ice rink it kept control with any of three dynamics models [S26 high]. The adaptation methods themselves are in 3.8.

Tuning and smoothness. Knobs are temperature (0.3 default), gamma (0.015), noise std and horizon [S1 high]. log-MPPI's normal log-normal noise cut the injected variance about 30% at equal or better success (92% to 100% and 84% to 96% in two forest scenarios) [S45 high][S45 medium]. Plain MPPI chatters; Savitzky-Golay on the noise can diverge and on the control delays the response, while SMPPI samples in derivative-of-action space, sets noise from the actuator rate limit, and completed corners all three baselines failed [S4 high]. Evidence is ground robots at 0.5 to 2 m/s on GPUs [S45 medium][S26 high]. The fastest MPPI in the record is a base policy driving a 1/10 car to 3.0 m/s on a slippery floor from an Intel NUC CPU with a 10-step, 100 ms horizon [S54 high]; nothing at 4.9 m/s in a 2.4 m box.

### 3.2 Gradient MPC and iLQR

Compute. The one measurement on the target CPU: an ACADO plus qpOASES NMPC with 16 states, 4 inputs and N=20 at 50 ms ran at 200 Hz on a Jetson Orin NX, mean 1.88 ms and max 3.17 ms, with box constraints only and obstacle avoidance in a separate planner [S47 medium]; its four thrust states are a first-order motor lag [S47 medium], the same structure as our taus. On weaker ARM cores, a sparse ADMM MPC-for-tracking QP with N=12, 3 states and a 20 ms sample time ran on a Raspberry Pi 3 at 2.99 ms average and 8.64 ms worst case [S43 high], with iteration counts spiking when the input bounds go active [S43 medium]; the worst case is about 3x the average (derived from [S43]), and that ratio is the tick-jitter risk any solver-based option carries into a 4 ms tick. With obstacles inside the solver, an ACADO chance-constrained MPC with two obstacles over a 25-step, 1.5 s horizon ran 16 ms mean and p75 under 22 ms on a Jetson TX2 [S48 high]. Differential-drive NMPC with N=30 and IPOPT took 12.5 to 15.8 ms median on a desktop i7-4770 against 5.9 ms for TEB, with no performance gain over TEB for simple differential drives [S17 high]. MPC-CBF at N=5 took 28 ms with IPOPT [S7 high]. Linear ADMM MPC with precomputed Riccati matrices runs at 500 Hz under 2 ms on a 168 MHz Cortex-M4 [S12 high], but needs fixed A, B, Q, R, so a traction refit regenerates the cache [S44 high]. iLQR has no embedded timing in the record; the SSL A-Team described a line-search iLQR with time-varying opponent PDFs in the cost, in future tense and without numbers [S23 high].

Moving obstacles. Time-dependent constraint sets under constant velocity [S17 high], half-spaces re-linearized each step [S12 high], KF-covariance-inflated ellipses [S8 high], or per-stage linearized chance constraints at 0.011 ms each [S49 medium]. A 97% per-stage chance constraint failed when other agents broke constant velocity: minimum distance 0.56 m against a 0.6 m limit [S49 high]. Infeasibility needs a fallback; one study decelerated on 2.8% of solves, worst gap 0.45 s [S49 medium].

Heading, tuning, smoothness. SO(2) needs increment operators inside the optimizer; Euclidean theta produced a wrong parking trajectory [S17 high]. A sector cost is nonconvex and nonsmooth, which drives solve time and infeasibility. The record holds two routes around that: second-order cone constraints solved by cached-Riccati ADMM on Cortex-M, the closest analogue to a cone-shaped keep-out [S44 high], and dynamic feedback linearization to a two-chain integrator, which turns the obstacle constraint into a QCQP but removes heading from it entirely [S11 high]. Knobs are Q/R plus constraint decay rates, and CBF gamma has no selection rule [S7 high]. Output is smooth when the solver converges, but solve time is not: the Pi 3 worst case ran about 3x its average when input bounds went active [S43 high].

Verdict. Plausible for N=20 with box constraints [S47 medium]. Disc constraints, a sector cost and the steer-brake nonlinearity together have no embedded timing in the record; the nearest point, two disc chance constraints on a TX2, ran 16 ms [S48 high]. They cost more to build and tune than rollouts over the same horizon, and a fixed candidate count has no solver jitter to budget for.

### 3.3 CBF and predictor-feedback safety filters

The existing velocity barrier, bound = gap / (tau_lin_d + delay_s) toward each hazard [R hazard_avoidance.md], is already a discrete-time barrier on the plant's stopping envelope. The literature adds three things. A predictive CBF over a horizon longer than the delay enforces constraints with the delay unmodeled and covers unmodeled first-order input dynamics: at gamma 0.8 a horizon of 8 steps violated constraints while 20 and 80 steps at 5 ms satisfied them [S9 high]. Predictor feedback forward-integrates the delay-free model over the delay with the stored inputs and recovers the delay-free response shifted by the delay; without it both state and input constraints were violated, and a robust variant survived a 50% delay underestimate that broke the plain predictor [S10 high]. CBF constraints inside MPC let N=5 match distance-constrained MPC at N=7, with gamma setting the margin (min distance 1.483 at gamma 0.1, 0.110 at gamma 0.5) [S7 high], but gamma is tuned by hand against infeasibility [S7 high][S11 high] and recursive feasibility is not guaranteed [S7 high]. D-CBF handles moving obstacles with KF-predicted centers and covariance-inflated extents at 10 Hz on an i5 NUC [S8 high]. The only CBF solve-time statement in the record is that the QCQP stays under the 50 ms sampling time on an i7-10750H laptop at N=8 to 14 [S11 medium]. Every CBF result here is simulation except S8, whose hardware run is qualitative and reports no solve time [S8 high][S9 high][S10 high][S11 medium].

Verdict. Keep the barrier as the final layer, extend it to a predictive form over delay plus a few lag constants [S9 high], and evaluate it on the predicted state [S10 high]. It filters commands; it does not choose the route.

### 3.4 Trajectory sampling (TIGERs-style bang-bang)

Compute. TIGERs generate bang-bang trajectories to the target and to a few intermediate targets, replanned every 10 ms. With 32 robots plus ball on a 12 x 9 m half field, 1000 random runs gave 0.3 ms average and 3.6 ms average maximum per iteration in the JVM, 0.9 and 6.1 ms in the wall-of-robots case, about 35,000 trajectories per robot per second [S20 high]. With all robots planned in parallel and the AI sharing the CPU, the average grew to 4 ms per robot per iteration with peaks to 60 ms including garbage collection [S20 high]. The 2024 version uses five random intermediate targets plus the previous frame's target, lazy first-acceptable-path selection, and check steps sized by distance to the nearest obstacle, and reports no timing [S14 high]. ER-Force and RobôCIn adopted it [S14 high].

Moving obstacles. Opponents are time-indexed reachable sets from 1D bang-bang trajectories under about 3 m/s and 3 m/s^2, capped at 0.5 s, shrunk to a tube along the velocity with zero dynamic radius for stationary robots; longer horizons inflate obstacles and lengthen paths [S14 high]. The 2019 lookahead was 3 s at 100 ms steps [S20 high]; 2024 cut it to 0.5 s [S14 high]. Obstacles are role-dependent: opponents come in two variants, constant velocity over a short window for aggressive roles and acceleration to max speed for cautious ones; own robots are modeled by their planned trajectory and the ball by its time-indexed trajectory [S14 high]. A velocity-dependent margin m = (min(vmax, v)/vmax)^2 x 0.2 m lets the robot pass close when slow [S14 high]. Two constant-velocity discs collide over a closed-form interval from one quadratic, no time stepping [S52 high].

Heading. TIGERs plan 2D translation only; orientation is controlled separately [S20 high]. A differential drive cannot do that, so our candidates must be command sequences through the plant, which supplies heading along the rollout for free.

Delay and lag. TIGERs assume the robot is on the previous trajectory and reset only on a large deviation [S20 high]. Asymmetric braking lives outside the planner in an emergency brake that brakes harder than the planner assumed [S14 medium]. The A-Team locks the first X seconds of the previous plan and replans from the state at T+X, trusting the follower over the delayed vision estimate [S23 high].

Adaptation, tuning, smoothness. Plant constants enter only through the candidate generator, so a refit is one struct write. Knobs are penalty weights (total time, a fixed 5 s collision penalty, 3x front-collision duration) and a hysteresis that keeps the previous target unless a new one is significantly better [S20 high]. The known weakness is that the path and its time estimate fluctuate between frames [S14 high], and greedy single-waypoint search fails on a wall of robots [S20 high]. Crash fouls fell from about 3 per match in 2023 to 1.8 in 2024 for the approach as a whole [S14 medium].

### 3.5 Reactive methods (DWA, pure pursuit, VO/ORCA)

DWA costs 20 to 58 ms per cycle on an i5-7200U against 3 to 6 ms for TEB [S16 high]; its constant control over the horizon cannot plan reversals [S17 high], it oscillates in narrow spaces, and its weights are hand-tuned [S16 high]. Pure pursuit and Regulated Pure Pursuit are geometric and model no lag or delay [G3 low]. An A* plus carrot follower kept 96.8% success at a 1.0 m/s cap and collapsed to 24.9% at 2.0 m/s because it cannot slow for tight passages [S55 high]. NH-ORCA restricts the holonomic velocity to a precomputed polygon rotated to the heading and solves a small QP; it is kinematic, assumes constant-velocity others, and falls back to spin-in-place [S18 high]. Capping omega below omega_max gives smoother trajectories and avoids those in-place rotations [S18 high]. DD-AVOCADO runs one step in 1.1 ms with 19 agents on a desktop Ryzen [S19 high] and cuts collision rates from 10 to 92% (AVOCADO and ORCA) to 0 to 25.6% in 3 x 3 m simulations [S19 medium], but its commands are discontinuous in v and omega [S19 high] and its precomputed set must be redone when vmax or wmax change [S19 medium]. The knobs are the tracking-error bound (E or eps_max), the heading-reach time T and the VO horizon tau [S18 high][S19 high]; the rectangular admissible-velocity approximation makes the QP suboptimal, and neither goal convergence nor freedom from deadlock is guaranteed [S18 high][S19 high]. NimbRo's approach-from-behind is an orientation-specific halo around the ball with straight and arc segments [S37 high]. Hardware validation is at 0.13 to 0.65 m/s [S18 high][S19 high].

Verdict. Pure pursuit is what `PursuitNavigation` already is. VO's constant-velocity model and hard mode switches lose to time-indexed disc growth against a track with 340 ms gaps.

### 3.6 Global layers (cost-to-go fields, lattice A*, RRT)

Fast marching gives a cost-to-go field with a speed profile; FM2 took 0.13 to 0.19 s on a 500 x 180 grid on unstated hardware [S31 high]. An Eikonal field cannot represent heading-dependent cost; that needs the Ordered Upwind Method, whose compute grows with anisotropy because anisotropic nodes widen the update stencil, and the directional cost must be continuous, differentiable and convex or paths zig-zag [S32 high]. The anisotropic field costs the heading of travel and omits turning cost [S32 high], and anisotropy above about 10 produced local minima unless the grid was refined [S32 medium], which bounds how sharp a sector cost the OUM can take. Hybrid-A* and State Lattice take 39 to 43 ms on a desktop Ryzen for 10,000 m^2 maps with 16 headings, cached-heuristic replans 5 ms or 1 to 3 ms, heading as per-primitive penalties, no time dimension [S28 medium]. A 4D lattice with D* Lite replanned at about 10 Hz on a 1.6 GHz rover CPU, with graduated fidelity cutting runtime over 10x at equal solution cost [S29 high]. Safe-interval A* needs fully known obstacle trajectories [S30 high]; SIPP-IP ran under 0.1 s on an i7-10700F up to 128 x 128 cells [S30 high] while time-stepped A* exceeded a 100M-node cap in numerous runs [S30 medium]. RRT-X spent 40 s building its graph before the robot moved, on a desktop, and is myopic to obstacles faster than the robot [S15 high]. A RoboMaster grid A* with a safe-distance term needed about 100 ms per plan on a 32 x 20 grid [S22 high]. A lazily built visibility graph over discs is the SSL global option [S13 medium]; ER-Force's position-only RRT needed discs stretched along velocity that then blocked their own targets [S51 high].

Verdict. The arena holds two or three discs [R hazard_avoidance.md], and the existing tangent layer is a one-edge visibility graph. No global layer.

### 3.7 Learned policies

Residual RL on pure pursuit transferred zero-shot to an F1TENTH car but needed its residual scale hand-set to 0.55 for 10 crash-free laps, with 8.38 ms inference on an i7 at 40 Hz [S53 high]. Residual RL on an MPPI base gave clear gains in simulation and essentially none on the real car; only model-based TD-MPC2 beat MPPI, SAC collapsed to do-nothing, PPO crashed [S54 high]. A three-layer PPO drift policy runs at 100 Hz on an Orin Nano [S56 medium], trained without motor dynamics but with previous commands in the observation [S56 low]. A LiDAR PPO navigation policy runs on an Orin NX at 10 Hz [S55 high]. Delay is handled by stacking history [S54 high] or not at all [S53 high][S55 high]. No policy in the record adapts to traction online.

Verdict. Not in the first version. A learned terminal cost-to-go for the rollout scorer is the one slot where it fits later.

### 3.8 Online plant adaptation

From cheapest: RLS on a two-lag differential-drive plant with a deadband, seeded from an offline fit, which cut run-to-run parameter spread about 10x versus random seeds [G10 low]; closed-form RLS on function-encoder coefficients, O(k^2) per step, converging in about 5 s [S26 high], though the encoder's forward call took 2.5 ms, about 8x the neural-ODE baseline (derived), which lowered the control rate [S26 medium]; per-cycle gradient fine-tuning from a meta-learned start, which held lateral error at 0.12 to 0.14 m across tire and surface changes where a parametric baseline crashed [S24 high] and cut cost 25% on a mixed-friction course [S25 high], but assumed known task boundaries [S25 high] and ran adaptation every 80 ms with the gradient step count capped at 14 for real time, below which the authors call it noise-vulnerable [S25 high]; a three-feature GP residual refit between laps [G8 low]; particle estimation of physical parameters inside the rollouts at 9.22 to 11.42 ms on a desktop RTX 5090 [S27 medium]. The sources disagree on gradient meta-learning: [S26 high] saw MAML gradients explode above 10^6 on streaming hardware data, while [S24 high] and [S25 high] report it working on RC cars. Two caveats: a parameter that does not affect observed motion is not learned [S27 high], and cached-matrix MPC cannot absorb a plant change without regeneration [S44 high][S12 high].

Verdict. Adapt two or three scalars of `JigPlantParams` (a gain scale on k_fwd and k_rev, the two linear taus) with RLS from EKF speed innovations, seeded from the jig fit [G10 low]. The estimator owns the adapted struct, since it has the innovations. `PlantConfiguration` is parsed once and handed to both `KalmanMotionEstimator` and the navigation so that a refit moves the estimator and the controller together [R plant_backed_control.md]; adaptation keeps that contract only if the planner's rollouts, the feedforward and `HazardAvoidanceSettings` (whose `max_linear_speed_fwd/rev` and `barrier_horizon_s` are copied from the fit at construction today [R hazard_avoidance.md]) all read the estimator's adapted struct each tick. A planner-local copy would put the estimator's prediction and the planner's rollout on different plants, the drift the `[plant]` table exists to prevent. On the dead-reckoning arm there are no innovations, so adaptation is disabled and the seeded fit stands.

## 4. What comparable domains do

RoboCup SSL. Fields are 12 x 9 m with robots near 3 m/s [S20 high][S14 high], overhead vision and off-board planning at 100 Hz [S20 high][S51 medium]. The champion runs trajectory sampling with time-indexed obstacles and a separate executing skill that owns emergency braking [S14 high]; ER-Force left RRT for the same reasons and still lists opponent prediction as unsolved [S51 high]; the A-Team adds the delay-locked replan and an IMM tracker with constant-velocity plus max-acceleration modes [S23 high]. What transfers: the planner-plus-executor split, time-indexed discs with a 0.5 s cap, hysteresis on the previous plan, and ignoring collisions about 1 s out [S14 medium]. What does not: the omnidirectional base that lets heading be planned separately [S20 high]; ours is coupled through c_sb [Q].

RoboMaster. Grid A* with an isotropic safe-distance repulsion beat a DQL waypoint policy 89 to 94% against 13 to 24%, ignoring opponent history [S22 high] and using the enemy's last-seen position when it is out of sight [S22 medium].

Small-scale racing and drifting. Model-based control wins and learning helps at the margins: RLPP beat its pure-pursuit base by about 6% but stayed 7.35% behind the best model-based tracker [S53 low]; residual RL on MPPI gave no real-world gain [S54 high]; meta-learned dynamics inside MPPI handled tire and surface changes [S24 high]. Actuator delay above roughly 95 ms caused spin-out in a drift controller until a forward-integrating predictor with a 170 ms horizon removed it [G4 low]. What transfers: predictor feedback and adaptation of a few plant terms.

Combat robotics. Published autonomy is thin. The WPI 3 lb project used a ceiling camera, ArUco tags, chase-and-bump with a gyro PID turn and a 10 to 20 ms Wi-Fi round trip, and reports no match [S38 medium]. Akron's 60 lb design was a fight-or-flight branch tested on simulated inputs [S39 medium]. Orbitron at BattleBots runs shared control (software steers, human throttles) with per-opponent targeting; its documented failures include perception blinding and unexplained wall collisions [S40 medium]. Chomp fired a hammer from lidar range [S42 medium]. No combat source reports a planner, so SSL is the closest primary evidence.

Pursuit-evasion. Directional pursuit is the goal we want, range to zero with relative bearing and LOS angle to zero, and a global feedback law exists for a kinematic unicycle with a speed advantage, at the price of a range overshoot during reorientation [S33 high]. The law is a handful of transcendental calls per tick [S33 low] and needs a bound on the evader's turn rate [S33 low]. The identical-cars game has closed-form bang-bang controls on its barrier [S34 high], the conic-sensor game is unsolved [S35 high], and a grid HJ solver could not resolve the capture set's thin edge [S36 low]. What transfers: the terminal cost shape, and the warning that a precomputed capture table is not a shortcut.

## 5. Candidate designs for the new navigation type

### Rank 1: PlantRolloutNavigation (structured trajectory sampling through the fitted plant, two-rate)

Every 4 ms tick. Start from the state where the next command bites: our state at t + delay_s, forward-integrated through the plant over the stick commands stored in [t - delay_s, t] (predictor feedback [S10 high]). The estimator already runs `JigPlantModel` over exactly that command history [R plant_backed_control.md], and the navigation cannot reach it: `NavigationInterface::update(RobotDescriptionsStamped, FieldDescription, const TargetSelection&)` carries no command history, `CommandRingBuffer command_history_` is private to `KalmanMotionEstimator`, and the buffer holds the transmitter's stick-space readback (`CommandFeedback.stick_commands`, after mixing, deadzones, saturation and `limit_linear_acceleration`), which a navigation-local copy of emitted `VelocityCommand`s would not reproduce. The plumbing is therefore one of two: the estimator publishes the delay-predicted `PlantState` (state at t + delay_s, integrated over the stored stick commands) on our `RobotDescription`, or `NavigationInterface::update` grows a command-span argument and the navigation integrates it. The first keeps one owner of the plant model and is the default here. From that state, read the active segment of the committed plan (a reference speed and heading with its switch time), pass it through the inverse-plant feedforward and speed PI from `MotionProfileNavigation`, then `HazardAvoidance::limit_command` against `hard_radius` as the final layer [R hazard_avoidance.md].

On dead reckoning. The Mr Stabs Mk2 profiles run `DeadReckoningMotionEstimator` because that robot has no jig fit, and the Mrs Buff Mk3 fit has not yet passed the EKF acceptance criteria even though the shared config selects the EKF arm [R plant_backed_control.md][R stage4_sim_report.md]. On a dead-reckoning arm our velocity is a function of the controller's own command, so the predictor collapses to open-loop feedforward and there are no speed innovations. Rank 1 then plans from the dead-reckoned pose with adaptation disabled. That is enough to run the sweep; the EKF arm is what makes the predictor and the RLS adaptation in 3.8 real.

Every perception frame (30 Hz), or every k-th tick. Build 50 to 150 candidates as two- or three-segment command sequences (segments of 0.1 to 0.25 s, 0.4 to 0.6 s total) covering turn-then-drive, arcs, reverse-then-turn, spin and brake, plus the previous best shifted one frame (hysteresis [S20 high]). Roll each through the plant at a 20 ms step, about 25 steps, with deadzones, asymmetric taus and steer-brake included. The EKF entry is the wrong primitive for this: `JigPlantModel::propagate` writes a 5x5 Jacobian by central finite differences (eleven propagations per call) and integrates exact arcs at a hardcoded 2 ms substep, and `propagate_unwrapped` drops the Jacobian but keeps the substep [R plant_backed_control.md]. The rollout needs a Jacobian-free stepper on `JigPlantParams`: `plant_steady_state()`, which already carries deadzone, steer-brake, droop and drift, then the sign-selected first-order lag (accelerating or decelerating tau by the sign of target minus current velocity) and one arc step per 20 ms. Score per step: time-indexed disc collision against every `FieldHazard` and the opponent footprint, with tracked centers advanced along filtered velocity and radii grown with lookahead (covariance coasting with a clamped velocity covariance [S48 high], or worst-case v_max x t [S50 high]); a weapon-sector cone cost on the opponent's predicted heading, this report's own design, whose weight decays with lookahead as the opponent's position distribution spreads [S23 high]; a velocity-dependent margin [S14 high]; and a terminal cost from range plus bearing and LOS alignment to the rear [S33 high]. The disc test must name its radius. `FieldHazard` carries three: the aim layers plan against `inflated_radius`, the barrier against `hard_radius`, and on the measured AER arena the opponent stood inside a fat disc for 48% of the match, which is why the speed rule is not keyed to it [R hazard_avoidance.md]. So a static-hazard hit on `inflated_radius` rejects the candidate before moving obstacles are checked [S14 high], and between `hard_radius` and `inflated_radius` the cost is soft, so a fight in the outer band stays legal, the same split the barrier already makes. Take the argmin, keeping the old plan unless the new one is significantly better [S20 high].

Plant fit. Rollouts use every fitted term. The feedforward inverts k_fwd, k_rev, k_ang, tau_lin_a/d, tau_ang_a/d and c_sb as today [R plant_backed_control.md]; the barrier horizon is tau_lin_d + delay_s [R hazard_avoidance.md]; the predictor uses delay_s. All three read the estimator-owned struct from 3.8, so a refit or an adaptation step moves them together.

Track dropout. Two cases. The house bot is a `HazardSource::TRACKED` hazard assembled from `Group::NEUTRAL` tracks: its disc grows per stale step and is dropped past `hazard_tracked_hold_s` [R hazard_avoidance.md]. The opponent is never a `FieldHazard`. Its footprint follows `is_stale`, flagged after 0.1 s unmeasured, and `coast()`, which integrates the constant-velocity model only up to `max_coast_s` (0.5 s in the shared config) and then holds position with growing covariance [R plant_backed_control.md]; the footprint disc grows with the covariance the opponent KF already grows, not by a separate rule. Past that, the terminal cost falls back to the last known pose, the last-seen position RoboMaster planners use when the enemy is out of sight [S22 medium], and the sector weight goes to zero.

Expected compute. 128 candidates x 25 steps = 3,200 plant steps. Nav2's CPU MPPI ran 12,800 rollout steps in 2.7 ms on an Orin Nano [S2 high], about 200 ns per step (derived), which puts 3,200 steps near 0.7 ms plus closed-form disc checks [S52 high]. That is a lower bound: Nav2's step is a kinematic differential-drive update plus a costmap lookup [S2 high], while one 20 ms step here is deadzone removal, the coupling clamp, the sign-selected lag and an arc. Experiment 3 measures the real per-step cost and sets K and N. For scale, 128 candidates at 30 Hz is 3,840 candidates per second against TIGERs' 35,000 closed-form bang-bang trajectories per robot per second [S20 high]; theirs are closed-form segments checked at adaptive steps, not 25-step plant integrations, so matching their throughput would need a candidate about ten times cheaper than a plant rollout.

Reuse. `HazardAvoidance` unchanged as the final layer; `MotionProfileNavigation::compensate_coupling` and its feedforward chain; `JigPlantParams` and `plant_steady_state()` for the rollout stepper, with the arc step written once for the rollout rather than borrowed from the 2 ms EKF integrator; `FieldHazard` discs as they are.

Risks. The predictor and the adaptation need the EKF arm, whose plant fit has not passed its acceptance criteria [R stage4_sim_report.md]; on a dead-reckoning profile the planner is open-loop from the dead-reckoned pose. Plan fluctuation between frames [S14 high]; a candidate set too coarse to thread two discs and the opponent; a sector cost that means nothing when the opponent spins faster than its heading can be tracked; a compute estimate borrowed from a lighter model in another codebase [S2 high].

### Rank 2: Small-K SMPPI with ancillary samples (same rollouts, different sampler)

Replace the structured set with K = 128 to 256 Gaussian perturbations in derivative-of-action space around the previous plan [S4 high], plus ancillary samples (brake, spin-to-face, the structured best) [S46 high], and a soft-max update with the cost redefined so the weights collapse to exp(-S/lambda) [S46 medium]. Predictor, rollout model, costs, tracker and barrier are Rank 1's. Compute at K=256, N=25: 6,400 steps, about 1.3 ms by the same scaling [S2 high], with the same lower-bound caveat. Gains: continuous refinement instead of a fixed menu, and smoothness by construction [S4 high]. Risks: the weighted average between two good modes lands in the bad one at small K [S5 high]; three more knobs [S1 high]; no CPU-only benchmark at this K and N in the record.

### Rank 3: Directional pursuit law plus existing layers (baseline)

A closed-form feedback in (rho, delta, gamma) drives range to zero with tail alignment [S33 high][S33 low], evaluated on the predicted state, then `steer_around` and `limit_command` as `MotionProfileNavigation` does today, with the barrier settings filled from k_fwd, k_rev, tau_lin_d and delay_s. `PursuitNavigation` runs only `steer_around` and `apply_reverse`; the barrier is stated in m/s against the plant fit, and `HazardAvoidanceSettings` with zero `max_linear_speed_fwd/rev` leaves it off [R hazard_avoidance.md]. Compute is negligible. It has no obstacle reasoning beyond the tangent layer, overshoots range during reorientation [S33 high], assumes a kinematic unicycle with a speed advantage [S33 high], and needs an opponent turn-rate bound [S33 low]. Build it as the baseline the sweep must beat and as the fallback when the rollout planner finds no acceptable candidate.

## 6. Experiments to run in the simulator before committing

1. Rear approach against a scripted turning opponent (constant yaw rate, then random reversals). Metric: fraction of runs reaching the rear sector within 3 s, and seconds spent inside the weapon cone. Compare Rank 3, `MotionProfileNavigation` and Rank 1.
2. Hazard clearance with a moving house bot and injected track gaps at the measured p90 of 340 ms [Q]. Metric: `fell_in`, `min_clearance_m`, `block_hits` from the sim's EPISODE line [R hazard_avoidance.md].
3. Compute on the Orin NX CPU with YOLO running: p50 and p99 of the replan and of the tick, swept over candidate count K (32 to 256) and step count N (15 to 30). Pass: p99 tick under 4 ms, replan inside one perception frame. This measures the real per-step cost of the rollout stepper and fixes the K and N the design ships with.
4. Command smoothness: mean |delta u / delta t| as in MRPB's metric [S16 high] and reversal count, against `MotionProfileNavigation`.
5. Traction degradation, on the EKF arm: scale k_fwd, k_rev and both linear taus by 0.6 to 0.8 mid-run. Metric: time to recover terminal error and rear-approach rate, with and without RLS adaptation, against the 5 s figure [S26 high].
6. Delay mismatch: sim delay 52 ms with the planner assuming 26 and 78 ms (the 50% error of [S10 high]). Metric: wall contacts and overshoot.
7. Horizon and disc growth: 0.3, 0.5 and 0.8 s with covariance against worst-case inflation. Metric: path length and collisions, expecting the TIGERs tradeoff [S14 high].
8. Candidate-set size: 32 to 256 candidates, and Rank 1 against Rank 2 at equal compute. Metric: success rate per millisecond.
9. Dead reckoning against EKF: run experiments 1 and 2 on both `our_robot_mode` arms. Metric: the gap in rear-approach rate and `min_clearance_m`, which is what the EKF acceptance buys the planner.

## 7. Open questions

- Which plumbing carries the delay-predicted state: a `PlantState` published on our `RobotDescription` by the estimator, or a command-span argument on `NavigationInterface::update`? The first keeps one plant-model owner; the second keeps the interface's inputs self-describing.
- Rank 1's predictor and the RLS adaptation need the EKF arm, and the Mrs Buff Mk3 fit has not passed the EKF acceptance criteria [R stage4_sim_report.md]. How much does the planner lose when that estimate is wrong, or when a profile dead-reckons (experiment 9)?
- The opponent filter is a 4-state constant-velocity KF [R plant_backed_control.md]; a weapon-sector cost needs predicted heading and yaw rate. Which estimator supplies them, and does the cost mean anything against an opponent spinning several times a second?
- Hard sector keep-out or decaying soft cost?
- Where does the replan run: in the 250 Hz thread every k-th tick, or a separate thread handing over plans, given the no-blocking rule?
- Which observable drives traction adaptation, and how fast must it converge within a match?
- Does the 52 ms delay hold at competition, and how much margin does the predictor need [S10 high]?
- How large a hysteresis penalty stops plan flapping without holding a bad plan?
- Hole geometry and house-bot inflation are still undecided [R hazard_avoidance.md].
